CARDIOVASCULAR ENGINEERING

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death and illness in developed countries and imposes a huge economic burden on our society. 

One of the indicators of cardiovascular health is the structural integrity of arterial walls. A well-known and widely reported method for assessing the physical state of the arterial wall is the noninvasive and relatively inexpensive brachial artery Flow-Mediated Dilation (FMD) test, in which blood flow through the brachial artery is obstructed temporally using a pressure cuff wrapped around the upper arm, causing the artery to become almost completely closed. The cuff is then suddenly deflated, allowing the flow to rush back in while the dilating artery is monitored until full recovery, using an ultrasound scanner. FMD metrics have been proposed in the past as potential cardiovascular health indicators. Correlations have been found linking abnormal FMD results with many underlying conditions and risk factors directly affecting cardiovascular health.  However, there is a significant lack of quantified metrics that relates the cardiovascular health with the blood vessel response during the FMD process, which prevents it from being an effective and pervasive diagnostic for CVDs.

​

The physics and mechanics of FMD are complex. This highly transient process occurs at two main time scales, including that of a heartbeat (pulsation period) and that of the artery's dilation soon after the uncuffing process. Since the artery walls are not rigid, flow conditions will influence the artery's diameter change, which will in turn affect the flow conditions, making the problem fully coupled via a two-way fluid-structure interaction. The most challenging aspect of this system stems from the fact that, the arterial wall's mechanical properties are not constant throughout the FMD process, due to a physiological phenomenon known as the mechanotransduction. Fig. 7 shows the response of the blood vessel during the FMD process. When the blood flow is abruptly allowed back through the shrunken artery, the drastic Wall shear stress (WSS) increase is picked up by the endothelial cells (ECs), initiating signal pathways that will stimulate the change of the mechanical property of the arterial wall. The artery's diameter would then respond by increasing under the fluid's pressure, and hence initiating dilation and decreasing the flow speed along with the WSS, which completes the feedback loop that makes FMD a self-modulating process. In collaboration with Dr. Sridhar Santhanam from Villanova University and Dr. Chandra Sandy Sehgal from the Department of Radiology of the University of Pennsylvania, my group is dedicated to the examination of the

pulsatile blood flow in large blood vessels and the mechanotransduction during the flow-mediated dilation process. We aim to seek quantitative metrics that characterize the physical state of blood vessels in both healthy and unhealthy conditions, via a rigorous development of physics-based models and experimentation. We have published three papers and submitted several grant applications on this topic (currently pending). A representative paper (Sidnawi, et al. 2020) is attached.

According to the Brain Injury Association, one brain injury occurs every nine seconds in the U.S. One of every sixty people lives with traumatic brain injury (TBI)-related disabilities, ranging from sight, hearing and/or memory loss, to depression and cognitive impairments, and more. Overall, brain injuries impose huge negative health and economic impacts on our society. Three big problems exit with respect to the current brain biomechanics research. (1). As the head is exposed to sudden external impacts, it is not clear how the soft brain enclosed in the hard skull is injured; (2). There is a lack of understanding about the role of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and arachnoid trabeculae (AT) in transmitting the impacts; (3) There is a gap between the brain biomechanics predictions and clinical diagnosis. To address these questions, I have developed three lines of research in brain biomechanics as summarized below:

(1). From egg yolk to brain, a biomimetic study using an artificial “Smart Brian”.

Similar to the soft brain matter bathed in the CSF that is enclosed in the skull, nature creates another model that has the same feature, a soft yolk bathed in the fluidic egg white that is enclosed in the egg shell, Fig. 8. Much of the research is inspired by our experiments using a Golden Goose Egg Scrambler®, in which one uses rotational force to scramble an egg inside of the eggshell. An interesting yet fundamentally important question is that, how can we damage or scramble the yolk without cracking the shell? The answer to this question will reveal the mystery of concussive brain injury. We have developed a novel biomimetic approach, namely a “Smart Brain” system, to examine the mechanisms that lead to the brain injury, as shown in Fig. 9. The system allows us to directly visualize the motion and deformation of the brain matter, when the head is exposed to external translational or rotational impacts.   The research has led to two U.S. patents, two manuscripts submitted to highly respected journals and one NSF proposal currently under review. It also peaked significant interest from the general public and featured on NBC 10®, https://www.nbcphiladelphia.com/news/local/smart-brain-could-help-doctors-see-the-full impact_philadelphia/1976707/, The Doctor’s Channel®, Philadelphia Magazine®, and American Society of Engineering Education First Bell Report, and Villanova Magazine,  https://www1.villanova.edu/university/sites/magazine/summer-2019/smart-brain.html.

(2). Transient Fluid-Structure-Interaction of CSF in the Subarachnoid Space (SAS).

The Arachnoid Trabeculae (AT) are complex honeycomb structures found in the SAS between the arachnoid mater and the pia mater, as shown in Fig. 10. A big problem with respect to the current brain biomechanics research is that, the critical role of the CSF flow through the porous AT in the SAS, has been overlooked. I believe that, concussive and sub-concussive injury occurs as a result of a series of force transmission between the rigid skull, the CSF and the soft brain matter. The impact duration is very short and thus the force transmission is extremely transient. In collaboration with Dr. Liyun Wang from University of Delaware, we are developing a comprehensive analytical, numerical and experimental

approach to examine the flow and pressurization of the CSF in the SAS, which has led to significantly improved understanding of the fluid-structure-interactions involved in incidence and mitigation of brain injury, and thus provides very critical insights regarding the head protection mechanisms. This research has led to three journal publications, with two others currently under review. We are also in the preparation of an NSF and an NIH proposal on this topic. A representative paper (Lang,Figure 11: MRI data acquisition, processing and analysis. et al., Phys. Fluids, 2017) is attached.

(3). From Biomechanics to clinical diagnosis:

Clinically, it is important to know where the weak point in brain is, so that targeted diagnosis and treatment of concussive and sub-concussive brain injury can be performed. In collaboration with Dr. Feroze Mohamed from Jefferson Integrated Magnetic Resonance Center of Thomas Jefferson University and Dr. Andrew Newberg from the Marcus Institute of Integrative Health of

Thomas Jefferson University, we have incorporated the biomechanics predictions of brain injury with MRI investigations, Figure 11, to reveal the structural damage as a result of impact on the head, paving the path for its clinical applications, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TNMKyWZFWZI. A journal paper has been submitted on this topic.

Transport in Biological Systems

I apply fundamental principles of fluid dynamics to the investigation of the transport phenomena in many other biological systems, ranging from bone, cartilage, skeletal muscle, kidney, and cells. For example, it is believed that, muscles and bones, serving as a

functional unit enabling body’s locomotion, communicate mechanically and biochemically during musculoskeletal development and maintenance. As muscle is electrically stimulated and muscle fibers contract, muscle-residing cells are found to secret many signaling factors (termed myokines). These myokines acts on neighboring organs, including bone, and are involved in bone formation and repair. Thus, it is critical to understand the transport processes of myokines from muscle to adjacent bone tissue. In collaboration with Dr. Liyun Wang from the University of Delaware, we are developing a comprehensive theoretical and experimental approach, to provide the first quantitative estimations of the hydraulic permeability of skeletal muscles and to explore the fluid flow inside the skeletal muscle sample during a compression process. The project helps us to understand the physical constrains in muscle-bone cross-talk and can potentially lead to new targets for treating musculoskeletal diseases and defects. A representative paper (Wang, et al., JMBB, 2020) is attached.
