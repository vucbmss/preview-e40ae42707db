Fluid-assisted Manufacturing-When Fluids “Meet” Nanomaterials

​In the past two and a half years, I started a new adventure by applying fluid mechanics principles in advanced manufacturing. The adventure started when I visited the Hybrid Nano-Architectures and Advanced Manufacturing Laboratory directed by Dr. Bo Li, Assistant Professor of Mechanical Engineering at Villanova.  The puzzle that Dr. Li brought up was that, although significant progress has been made, current nanomanufacturing is still like cooking. You throw different ingredients into the pot and see how it comes out. There are plenty of uncertainties and a significant lack of understanding of the fundamental physics/chemistry behind the “cooking” process. I grasped the tremendous potential of applying fluid mechanics principles to tackle these problems. This meeting of completely different fields led to close collaboration of the two groups and the ambition of creating of a new field of fluid-assisted manufacturing. Currently, we are collaborating on five different projects as summarized as follows.

01

Fluidic-assisted Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) growth of nanomaterials

Chemical vapor deposition has been used extensively for synthesizing two-dimensional (2D) materials, due to its low cost and promise for high-quality monolayer crystal synthesis. One of the key reasons behind the uncertainty of the CVD process is that it involves chemical reactions coupled with complex fluid mixing where the transfer of momentum, heat, and mass significantly affects the reaction process and thereafter the final product. Using computational fluid mechanics predictions, coupled with novel design of the CVD system based on the key insights of fluid flow, we are

investigating the fundamental physical and chemical processes of CVD, Figure 16. This research may establish a new fluidic perspective of CVD theory and revolutionize the way of designing future CVD setup. An NSF proposal is under review. A representative paper (Zhou, et al., ACS Applied Materials and Interface, 2020) is attached.

02

Fluidic-assisted sorted assembly of nanomaterials

Nanomaterials are usually synthesized with a wide size distribution. To achieve stable and repeatable device performance, size sorting is usually required which not only significantly increase the cost, but also reduce the productivities. We bring out a new concept of fluidic-assisted sorted assembly to achieve simultaneous nanomaterial sorting and assembly at the solution-polymer interface. In Figure 17, a nanoparticle sorting system is designed, similar to a viscometer that is widely

used in fluids but not in manufacturing research area. Advanced fluidic design and analytical tools are used to disclose the fundamental physics, design the assembly setup and predict the assembly results. This research has the potential to transform the flexible electronics manufacturing where the “cheap” raw nanomaterials (with wide size distribution) can be made into high-quality devices (with narrow size distribution) in a one-step assembly. We have published one paper on this topic (Zhu, eta., Langmuir , 2020). Recently, we secured a $500k NSF grant for “Sorting and Assembly of Nanomaterials on Polymer Substrates Using Fluidic and Weak Ultrasound Fields for Fabrication of Flexible Electronic Devices”. In this grant, we are taking a unique approach to developing a highly efficient sorting-assembly manufacturing process that selectively assembles nanomaterials with similar sizes into nanostructures. To achieve precise flow control, I propose to use a flow chamber system that is used in my lab for cellular biomechanics research, in the nanomanufacturing process. I also proposed analytical models that are typically applied in drug delivery research to precisely describe the transport, deposition and assembly of nanoparticles in the fluidic-assisted systems. The goal is to uncover the underlying physics of nanomanufacturing process.

03

Manufacturing of nanomaterial-based coatings and devices on polymer substrate

Nanomaterial-based coatings on polymer substrate that integrate the functionality of nanomaterials and the flexibility of polymer substrates promise to revolutionize a wide range of industries, from electronics and energy to healthcare. The high-resolution requirements and potential health and environmental concern of using nanomaterials and toxic solvents present significant challenges for next-generation manufacturing technologies. My group has developed a physics-based statistic model to quantify the deposition of nanoparticles on soft polymer substrates. The approach has been validated by coating experiments performed by Dr. Li’s group, e.g. a dip coating experiment shown in Fig. 18. We have submitted two journal papers on this topic, as well as an NSF proposal which is currently under review.

04

Binder jet 3D printing of porous metal and ceramic powders

Binder jetting technology is a disruptive method of 3D printing in which an industrial printhead deposits a liquid binder onto a thin layer of powdered particles, layer by layer, until an object is formed. As one of the fastest methods of 3D printing, binder jetting is regarded as having promise as a production technology. However, each powdered material has its own characteristics, and particles that have porous surfaces are more difficult to binder jet together than those with

smooth surfaces. The team led by Dr. Bo Li and myself have recently secured a Pennsylvania Manufacturing Initiative to investigate how to best wet porous particles with binder and generate guidelines or parameters for this form of 3D printing, as shown in Fig. 19. We are working with a leading metal 3D printing company, ExOne, to develop improved 3D printing processes. This transformative project allows us to expand from fundamental science to industrial, practical applications.
